The streaming setup and reward

Each episode streams 8 chunks of 4 seconds, choosing either 2.85 or 4.3 Mbps for each chunk. Downloading a chunk adds playable video to the buffer; playback drains it. If downloading takes longer than the available buffer, playback stalls. Buffer occupancy is shown from 0% to 100%, with 100% equal to 60 seconds.

The simulator uses 7 constant-bandwidth network traces: 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5, 5.5, and 6 Mbps. Holding these conditions fixed makes the relationship between rewards, training, and behavior easier to inspect.

Reward per chunk = bitrate / 4.3 − stall penalty × stall seconds / 4

Higher bitrate earns more reward; stalling subtracts reward. Bitrate is in Mbps. The heatmap shows expected return: the sum of rewards over an episode, averaged across the seven traces. Within each trace, all possible bitrate sequences are weighted by their probabilities under the policy.

How reinforcement learning updates the policy

The policy has two trainable parameters: weight w and bias b. Together they determine the probability of choosing high bitrate at each buffer level:

P(high bitrate) = sigmoid(w × buffer seconds / 60 + b)

Each training step samples 16 streaming episodes. REINFORCE, a policy-gradient method, uses the rewards that follow each choice to estimate an update. There are no examples of the heuristic’s decisions to imitate.

ĝ = average of Gt ∇θ log πθ(at | st)

Here θ = (w, b), π is the policy, and Gt is the total reward from step t onward. This is the core gradient estimate; the implementation also adds a small entropy bonus (0.01) to encourage exploration and uses Adam to update the parameters.

Training is computed anew in your browser. The heatmap evaluates a grid of policies; it does not store their training paths. This small policy and short simulator make both computations fast. Every run has 10,000 updates and a fixed random seed, so identical settings and starting points reproduce the same trajectory.

What this litmus test can tell us

Heuristic rediscovery is a diagnostic check. Before asking RL for a better controller, test whether it can recover a familiar one. The full paper asks for appropriate behavior, performance comparable to the heuristic, and reliability across runs.

Optimization problem: a useful policy may exist, but training misses it. The landscape has ridges and plateaus; neither a small policy nor gradient ascent guarantees a good solution.

Objective problem: training may successfully optimize a reward that favors the wrong behavior. Changing the learning rate cannot fix an incentive to ignore stalls.

The labels below the plots describe the selected policy’s preferences. Conservative and aggressive are descriptions, not automatic failures. Recovering a buffer-based pattern is useful evidence, but this demo does not compare performance against the paper’s heuristic baseline or test reliability across random seeds.

Training and evaluation use the same controlled traces. A successful run here does not establish performance on real networks. Read the paper and summary for the broader method and experiments.
